# 19 — Harappan architecture: what the era-0 pieces are drawn from

The first City Kit pass gave the Indus cities conical thatch roofs, curved
tiles, carved brackets and painted borders with peacocks on them. That is a
building idiom roughly a thousand years and a thousand miles away from
Dholavira. This note records what the era-0 pieces were redrawn from, so the
next person changing them does not have to find it again — and so the two
places where the evidence is genuinely uncertain stay marked as uncertain.

Everything here is **Itihaas**: it is what excavation shows, not a story. Where
scholars disagree, the disagreement is the content.

## The facts the art is built on

**Brick, standardised.** Harappan brick is famously proportioned 1 : 2 : 4 —
thickness to width to length — so a brick bonds with its neighbour whichever
way it is laid. Roughly 28 × 14 × 7 cm at the large cities and 24 × 12 × 6 cm
at smaller ones, and the ratio holds almost everywhere in the Harappan zone.
Mud brick for most of a town; baked brick where water touches it — drains,
bathing floors, wells, plinths.

**Material follows the ground.** Baked brick at Harappa, mud brick at
Kalibangan, and dressed stone at Dholavira, which had stone and no river clay.
Dholavira's reservoirs are stone masonry over mud-brick cores.

**Flat roofs.** Wooden beams carrying reed matting and packed mud, with a low
parapet. The roof is a room: pots, cots, work. No thatch, no tile, no curve.

**The courtyard is the house.** Rooms on all four sides of a small open court,
which is where light and air come from — outer walls carry few or no windows.

**The door is on the side lane**, not the street front.

**A stair to the roof**, often built on the outside of the wall.

**A bathing floor** in one corner, draining under the wall into a covered
street drain. The street drains are corbelled so they can run under a street
without collapsing, with inspection openings and soak pits.

**Wells of wedge-shaped brick**, cut to the curve so each course rings the
shaft exactly. Thirty were recorded at Harappa. No canopy, no pillars, no
carving: a well is a worn brick ring with rope grooves in its rim.

**No columns, no arches, no domes.** Corbelling was known and used for drains
and culverts, not as an architectural display. And no temple has been
identified anywhere in the Harappan world.

## The two things this kit deliberately does not assert

**The "granary" is contested.** The great platformed buildings at Harappa and
Mohenjo-daro were named granaries by their early excavators on an analogy with
Roman horrea. No charred grain, no storage jars and no clay sealings were found
in them, and the analogy has no parallel in any South Asian building tradition.
Most archaeologists now decline the identification and call them great halls or
simply large public buildings. **So does this kit**: the era-0 piece is
`bd-har-hall`, a *Great hall*, and its card says plainly that what it held is
still argued about. The game keeps a separate, humbler `bd-har-store` — raised
mud-brick blocks with goods stacked over them — for the storage mechanic.

**Lothal's basin.** Widely described as a dockyard, and that is how the kit
draws it, but the identification has been argued over since it was dug. If a
reviewer wants the card softened, that is a legitimate change, not a
correction.
